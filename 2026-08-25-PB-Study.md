# Problem Setup

## The observed data

We observe $n$ independent copies of the random record

$$
(X,W,A,Y).
$$

- $X$ is a possibly high-dimensional vector of observed pretreatment covariates;
- $W$ is a possibly high-dimensional observed pretreatment measurement;
- $A\in\{0,1\}$ is a binary treatment indicator, where $A=1$ denotes treatment and $A=0$ denotes no treatment;
- $Y$ is the observed outcome measured after treatment.

We also allow an unobserved pretreatment variable $U$. It may be scalar or multidimensional. The variable $U$ represents information that can affect treatment selection and the outcome but is not recorded in the observed data.

## The proxy

The pretreatment measurement $W$ may contain several blocks:

$$
W=(W_1,\ldots,W_J).
$$

Here, $J$ is the number of blocks. A block $W_j$ can be a random variable or a random vector. Depending on the application, it may be one repeated measurement, one sensor channel, one laboratory panel, one image region, or another prespecified group of measurements. The division into blocks records the internal structure of the high-dimensional measurement. We call $W$ a *candidate proxy* because some or all of its blocks may contain information about the hidden pretreatment variable $U$. 

## Potential outcomes and consistency

For each $a\in\{0,1\}$, let $Y(a)$ denote the outcome that would be observed if treatment were set to $a$.

### Assumption 1: Consistency and integrability

$$
Y=Y(A),
\qquad
\mathbb E|Y(a)|<\infty
\quad\text{for }a\in\{0,1\}.
\tag{A1}
$$

## The causal target

Our target is the average treatment effect in the target population:

$$
\tau\triangleq
\mathbb E\{Y(1)-Y(0)\}.
$$



## Research question

The project asks the following question:

> When a high-dimensional pretreatment candidate proxy $W$ is observed in addition to ordinary covariates $X$, what are sufficient assumptions/conditions to identify and estimate the population average treatment effect $\tau$, even though the unmeasured confounding $U$ remains hidden?

This question deliberately separates what is observed from what must later be assumed. The observed record contains $(X,W,A,Y)$. The causal target depends on the unobserved pair $Y(1),Y(0)$. The hidden variable $U$ explains why ordinary observational comparisons may fail. No method-specific identifying condition has yet been imposed.

## Notation recap

| Symbol | Meaning |
|---|---|
| $n$ | number of independently sampled units |
| $X$ | observed pretreatment covariates |
| $W=(W_1,\ldots,W_J)$ | high-dimensional pretreatment candidate proxy, divided into $J$ blocks |
| $W_j$ | the $j$th prespecified proxy block |
| $U$ | unobserved pretreatment variable |
| $A\in\{0,1\}$ | binary treatment |
| $Y$ | observed post-treatment outcome |
| $Y(1),Y(0)$ | potential outcomes under treatment and no treatment |
| $\tau=\mathbb E\{Y(1)-Y(0)\}$ | target-population average treatment effect |

# Related Literature

This project belongs to the literature on causal inference with proxy variables for unmeasured confounding. The closest approaches use different proxy structures and identify different causal targets. No method uniformly dominates the others, so their assumptions should be compared as complete identification packages rather than ranked by one condition in isolation.

Standard proximal causal inference uses two scientifically distinct proxy roles for the hidden variable $U$: a treatment-inducing proxy and an outcome-inducing proxy. Its identification theory combines latent proxy restrictions with an outcome or treatment bridge and a completeness condition. This framework provides a mature route to the population average treatment effect, but bridge existence is a nonautomatic range condition, and bridge estimation may be an ill-posed inverse problem. See [Miao, Geng, and Tchetgen Tchetgen (2018)](https://doi.org/10.1093/biomet/asy038), [Tchetgen Tchetgen et al. (2024)](https://doi.org/10.1214/23-STS911), and [Cui et al. (2024)](https://doi.org/10.1080/01621459.2023.2191817).

COCA and Single Proxy Control instead use one negative-control outcome that contains information about the untreated potential outcome $Y(0)$. Their central shielding condition states that this proxy is independent of treatment after conditioning on $Y(0)$ and $X$. A bridge then recovers the treated units' mean untreated outcome. The default nonparametric target is the effect on the treated, also called the ATT or ETT, rather than the population average treatment effect. The shielding condition is generally untestable, and bridge existence is again a nonautomatic range condition, although uniqueness of the bridge is not required once every valid solution yields the same target. See [Tchetgen Tchetgen (2014)](https://doi.org/10.1093/aje/kwt303) and [Park, Richardson, and Tchetgen Tchetgen (2024)](https://doi.org/10.1093/biomtc/ujae027).

Our cross-view approach is designed for a different data regime. A high-dimensional candidate proxy is divided into prespecified blocks. A representation is learned from all blocks except one, while the held-out block is used to measure treatment balance. Exact observed balance is converted into identification of the population average treatment effect by cross-view structural assumptions and outcome-relevant completeness. Approximate balance has a causal interpretation only under an additional quantitative stability condition. The final causal calculation uses ordinary adjustment by the learned representation rather than an explicitly estimated proximal bridge.

| Approach | Primary target | Identification mechanism | Main practical cost |
|---|---|---|---|
| Standard proximal inference | Population ATE | Proxy bridge plus completeness | Two defensible proxy roles and potentially ill-posed bridge estimation |
| COCA / Single Proxy Control | ATT or ETT | A proxy for $Y(0)$ plus shielding and a bridge | Outcome-specific proxy validity and a nonautomatic bridge condition |
| Cross-view proxy balancing | Population ATE | Held-out balance plus cross-view assumptions and outcome-relevant completeness | Valid view separation, attainable balance, and stability calibration for approximate claims |

The broad use of a single proxy for causal identification is not new, and we do not claim otherwise. The proposed contribution is the combination of high-dimensional cross-view representation learning, an observable conditional-balance endpoint, ordinary adjustment for the population average treatment effect, and a sensitivity certificate for approximate balance. A fuller assumption-by-assumption comparison and source audit is retained in [literature_review.md](literature_review.md).

# Identification

## Notations, Preliminaries, Setup

### Construction for a fixed block

Fix one block index $j\in\{1,\ldots,J\}$. Recall that $W=(W_1,\ldots,W_J)$ is the full pretreatment candidate proxy. We write

$$
W_{-j}
\triangleq
(W_1,\ldots,W_{j-1},W_{j+1},\ldots,W_J)
$$

for all proxy blocks except $W_j$. Such excluded block is called the held-out proxy block. 

### Representation Function Class 

We define the representation function class as 
$$
\Phi_j \triangleq \{\phi: \mathcal{X} \times \mathcal{W}_{-j} \longrightarrow \{1,\cdots,M_j\}\}.
$$

We use a finite-valued representation so that balance, overlap, and causal adjustment can be stated within explicit groups, called strata below, and estimated by ordinary stratified methods. This is a theoretical and computational choice. It does not assume that the hidden variable $U$ is discrete. Extending the same ideas to a continuous representation requires conditional-distribution versions of the objects defined below.

We fix the map as follow: 
$$
\phi_j \in \Phi_j, 
\qquad
Z_j\triangleq\phi_j(X,W_{-j}).
$$

Each $z\in \{1,\ldots,M_j\}$ satisfying

$$
P(Z_j=z)>0
\tag{PM}
$$

is a positive-mass stratum. Each such value is called retained. Conditional laws given only $Z_j=z$ are then well-defined.


### Latent distributions and the held-out proxy channel

Fix $a\in\{0,1\}$ and a retained representation value $z$ satisfying

$$
P(A=a,Z_j=z)>0.
$$

We define the treatment-arm-specific conditional distribution of $(X,U)$:

$$
Q_{a,j,z}
\triangleq
\mathcal L(X,U\mid A=a,Z_j=z).
\tag{Q}
$$

Next, for every measurable set $C\subseteq\mathcal W_j$, define

$$
K_j(C\mid x,u)
\triangleq
P(W_j\in C\mid X=x,U=u).
\tag{K}
$$

The object $K_j$ is the conditional measurement law of the held-out proxy block $W_j$ given $(X,U)$. We now define the operator induced by this conditional law. For any probability distribution $Q$ on $(X,U)$ and any measurable set $B\subseteq\mathcal X\times\mathcal W_j$, let

$$
(\mathcal K_jQ)(B)
\triangleq
\int
\left[
\int
\mathbf 1\{(x,w_j)\in B\}
K_j(dw_j\mid x,u)
\right]
Q(dx,du).
\tag{KO}
$$

The distribution $\mathcal K_jQ$ is obtained by the following two-step procedure. First, draw $(X,U)$ from $Q$. Second, draw $W_j$ from $K_j(\cdot\mid X,U)$. The resulting pair $(X,W_j)$ has distribution $\mathcal K_jQ$.

For each $a\in\{0,1\}$, define the conditional potential-outcome mean

$$
\mu_{a,j,z}(x,u)
\triangleq
\mathbb E\{Y(a)\mid X=x,U=u,Z_j=z\}.
\tag{MU}
$$



## Assumptions 

### Assumption 2: Joint latent exchangeability for the representation inputs

Fix the index for the held-out proxy block as $j$. For each treatment level $a\in\{0,1\}$, assume

$$
A\perp\!\!\!\perp\bigl(Y(a),W_{-j}\bigr)\mid(X,U).
\tag{A2}
$$

Once the observed covariates $X$ and hidden pretreatment variable $U$ are fixed, treatment $A$ contains no additional information about either the potential outcome $Y(a)$ or the representation inputs $W_{-j}$.

### Assumption 3: Separation of the held-out proxy block

Assume

$$
W_j\perp\!\!\!\perp(A,W_{-j})\mid(X,U).
\tag{A3}
$$

After conditioning on $(X,U)$, the measurement law of the held-out block $W_j$ is the same across treatment groups and does not depend on the other proxy blocks.

### Assumption 4: Representation overlap and integrability

For every retained $z$, assume

$$
0<P(A=1\mid Z_j=z)<1.
\tag{A4}
$$

This is representation overlap. Both treatment arms must occur with positive probability at every retained value of $Z_j$. Otherwise, the two treatment groups cannot be compared at that value.

### Admissible latent distributions

For each $z$ such that $P(Z_j=z)>0$, define

$$
S_{j,z}
\triangleq
\operatorname{supp}\{\mathcal L(X,U\mid Z_j=z)\}.
$$

Thus, $S_{j,z}$ is the support of $(X,U)$ among units whose representation equals $z$.

Let

$$
\mathcal Q_{j,z}
\triangleq
\left\{
Q:
\begin{array}{l}
Q\text{ is a probability distribution on }(X,U),\\
Q(S_{j,z})=1,\text{ and}\\
\displaystyle
\int|\mu_{a,j,z}(x,u)|\,Q(dx,du)<\infty
\text{ for }a\in\{0,1\}
\end{array}
\right\}.
$$


Thus, $\mathcal Q_{j,z}$ contains all integrable probability distributions of $(X,U)$ supported on $S_{j,z}$.

We require
$$
Q_{0,j,z},Q_{1,j,z}\in\mathcal Q_{j,z}.
$$

For any $Q\in\mathcal Q_{j,z}$, we use the shorthand

$$
\mathbb E_Q\{\mu_{a,j,z}(X,U)\}
\triangleq
\int\mu_{a,j,z}(x,u)\,Q(dx,du).
$$


### Assumption 5: Exact outcome-relevant completeness

For every $Q,Q'\in\mathcal Q_{j,z}$ and every $a\in\{0,1\}$,

$$
\mathcal K_jQ=\mathcal K_jQ'
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\mathbb E_Q\{\mu_{a,j,z}(X,U)\}
=
\mathbb E_{Q'}\{\mu_{a,j,z}(X,U)\}.
\tag{A5}
$$

Assumption 5 requires two latent distributions that are indistinguishable through the held-out proxy channel to agree on the mean of each potential outcome. It does not require the two latent distributions themselves to be equal.

## Exact Identification

### Lemma 1: Conditioning safety

Under Assumption 2, for each $a\in\{0,1\}$,

$$
Y(a)\perp\!\!\!\perp A\mid(X,U,Z_j).
$$

Under Assumption 3,

$$
W_j\perp\!\!\!\perp(A,Z_j)\mid(X,U).
$$

Consequently, for every measurable set $C$,

$$
P(W_j\in C\mid X,U,A,Z_j)
=
K_j(C\mid X,U)
$$

almost surely.

#### Proof

Recall that

$$
Z_j=\phi_j(X,W_{-j})
$$

is a measurable function of $(X,W_{-j})$.

Assumption 2 states that

$$
A\perp\!\!\!\perp\bigl(Y(a),W_{-j}\bigr)\mid(X,U).
$$

Because $Z_j$ is determined by $(X,W_{-j})$, this joint independence implies

$$
A\perp\!\!\!\perp\bigl(Y(a),Z_j\bigr)\mid(X,U).
$$

The weak-union property of conditional independence then gives

$$
Y(a)\perp\!\!\!\perp A\mid(X,U,Z_j).
$$

Next, Assumption 3 states that

$$
W_j\perp\!\!\!\perp(A,W_{-j})\mid(X,U).
$$

Because $Z_j$ is a measurable function of $(X,W_{-j})$, it follows that

$$
W_j\perp\!\!\!\perp(A,Z_j)\mid(X,U).
$$

Therefore, conditioning additionally on $(A,Z_j)$ does not change the conditional distribution of $W_j$ once $(X,U)$ is given:

$$
P(W_j\in C\mid X,U,A,Z_j)
=
P(W_j\in C\mid X,U).
$$

The right-hand side is $K_j(C\mid X,U)$ by definition. $\square$

### Definition 1: Adjustment functional

For the fixed representation $Z_j$, define

$$
\tau_{Z_j}
\triangleq
\sum_z P(Z_j=z)
\left[
\mathbb E(Y\mid A=1,Z_j=z)
-
\mathbb E(Y\mid A=0,Z_j=z)
\right].
$$

The sum is taken over all values $z$ satisfying $P(Z_j=z)>0$.

The quantity $\tau_{Z_j}$ is the treatment contrast obtained by adjusting for $Z_j$. It is an observed-data functional because it depends only on the joint distribution of $(Y,A,Z_j)$.

At this point, we do not assert that

$$
\tau_{Z_j}=\tau.
$$

The next theorem gives an exact expression for the difference between these two quantities.

For each representation value $z$, define

$$
p_{j,z}
\triangleq
P(Z_j=z)
$$

and

$$
\pi_{j,z}
\triangleq
P(A=1\mid Z_j=z).
$$

Assumption 4 guarantees that

$$
0<\pi_{j,z}<1
$$

whenever $p_{j,z}>0$.

### Theorem 1: Exact bias decomposition

Under Assumptions 1, 2, and 4,

$$
\begin{aligned}
\tau_{Z_j}-\tau
=
\sum_z p_{j,z}
\Bigg[
&(1-\pi_{j,z})
\left\{
\int \mu_{1,j,z}(x,u)\,Q_{1,j,z}(dx,du)
-
\int \mu_{1,j,z}(x,u)\,Q_{0,j,z}(dx,du)
\right\}
\\
&+
\pi_{j,z}
\left\{
\int \mu_{0,j,z}(x,u)\,Q_{1,j,z}(dx,du)
-
\int \mu_{0,j,z}(x,u)\,Q_{0,j,z}(dx,du)
\right\}
\Bigg].
\end{aligned}
\tag{BD}
$$

Thus, the bias of adjustment by $Z_j$ is determined by two treatment-arm differences. The first difference concerns the conditional mean of $Y(1)$. The second concerns the conditional mean of $Y(0)$.

#### Proof

Fix $a\in\{0,1\}$ and a representation value $z$ with $p_{j,z}>0$.

Consistency gives

$$
Y=Y(a)
\qquad\text{among units with }A=a.
$$

Therefore,

$$
\mathbb E(Y\mid A=a,Z_j=z)
=
\mathbb E\{Y(a)\mid A=a,Z_j=z\}.
$$

By iterated expectation,

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbb E\{Y(a)\mid A=a,Z_j=z\}
&=
\int
\mathbb E\{Y(a)\mid X=x,U=u,A=a,Z_j=z\}
\,Q_{a,j,z}(dx,du).
\end{aligned}
$$

Lemma 1 gives

$$
Y(a)\perp\!\!\!\perp A\mid(X,U,Z_j).
$$

Hence,

$$
\mathbb E\{Y(a)\mid X=x,U=u,A=a,Z_j=z\}
=
\mathbb E\{Y(a)\mid X=x,U=u,Z_j=z\}.
$$

The right-hand side is $\mu_{a,j,z}(x,u)$. Consequently,

$$
\mathbb E(Y\mid A=a,Z_j=z)
=
\int
\mu_{a,j,z}(x,u)\,
Q_{a,j,z}(dx,du).
\tag{6}
$$

Applying equation (6) to both treatment arms gives

$$
\begin{aligned}
\tau_{Z_j}
=
\sum_z p_{j,z}
\Bigg[
&
\int\mu_{1,j,z}(x,u)\,Q_{1,j,z}(dx,du)
\\
&-
\int\mu_{0,j,z}(x,u)\,Q_{0,j,z}(dx,du)
\Bigg].
\end{aligned}
\tag{7}
$$

We next express the true causal effect within each representation value. By the law of total expectation over $A$,

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbb E\{Y(a)\mid Z_j=z\}
={}&
\pi_{j,z}\,
\mathbb E\{Y(a)\mid A=1,Z_j=z\}
\\
&+
(1-\pi_{j,z})\,
\mathbb E\{Y(a)\mid A=0,Z_j=z\}.
\end{aligned}
$$

Lemma 1 applies to either treatment arm. Therefore,

$$
\mathbb E\{Y(a)\mid A=1,Z_j=z\}
=
\int\mu_{a,j,z}(x,u)\,Q_{1,j,z}(dx,du)
$$

and

$$
\mathbb E\{Y(a)\mid A=0,Z_j=z\}
=
\int\mu_{a,j,z}(x,u)\,Q_{0,j,z}(dx,du).
$$

It follows that

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbb E\{Y(a)\mid Z_j=z\}
={}&
\pi_{j,z}
\int\mu_{a,j,z}(x,u)\,Q_{1,j,z}(dx,du)
\\
&+
(1-\pi_{j,z})
\int\mu_{a,j,z}(x,u)\,Q_{0,j,z}(dx,du).
\end{aligned}
\tag{8}
$$

The true average treatment effect is

$$
\tau
=
\sum_z p_{j,z}
\left[
\mathbb E\{Y(1)\mid Z_j=z\}
-
\mathbb E\{Y(0)\mid Z_j=z\}
\right].
\tag{9}
$$

Substituting equation (8) into equation (9), subtracting the result from equation (7), and collecting the coefficients of the two treatment-arm distributions gives

$$
\begin{aligned}
\tau_{Z_j}-\tau
=
\sum_z p_{j,z}
\Bigg[
&(1-\pi_{j,z})
\left\{
\int\mu_{1,j,z}\,dQ_{1,j,z}
-
\int\mu_{1,j,z}\,dQ_{0,j,z}
\right\}
\\
&+
\pi_{j,z}
\left\{
\int\mu_{0,j,z}\,dQ_{1,j,z}
-
\int\mu_{0,j,z}\,dQ_{0,j,z}
\right\}
\Bigg].
\end{aligned}
$$

This is equation (BD). $\square$

Theorem 1 isolates the exact obstacle to adjustment by $Z_j$. Adjustment is unbiased if the two treatment arms agree on the conditional expectations of both potential outcomes within every representation value. Equality of the entire latent distributions is sufficient for this conclusion, but it is not necessary.

### Lemma 2: Cross-view factorization

Under Assumption 3, for each $a\in\{0,1\}$ and each $z$ satisfying $P(A=a,Z_j=z)>0$,

$$
\boxed{
\mathcal L(X,W_j\mid A=a,Z_j=z)
=
\mathcal K_jQ_{a,j,z}.
}
\tag{CF}
$$

#### Proof

Let $B$ be any measurable subset of the joint space of $(X,W_j)$. By iterated expectation,

$$
\begin{aligned}
&P\{(X,W_j)\in B\mid A=a,Z_j=z\}
\\
&=
\int
P\{(X,W_j)\in B
\mid
X=x,U=u,A=a,Z_j=z\}
\,Q_{a,j,z}(dx,du).
\end{aligned}
$$

Once $X=x$ is fixed, the inner probability can be written as

$$
\int
\mathbf 1\{(x,w_j)\in B\}
P(dw_j\mid X=x,U=u,A=a,Z_j=z).
$$

Lemma 1 gives

$$
P(dw_j\mid X=x,U=u,A=a,Z_j=z)
=
K_j(dw_j\mid x,u).
$$

Therefore,

$$
\begin{aligned}
&P\{(X,W_j)\in B\mid A=a,Z_j=z\}
\\
&=
\int
\left[
\int
\mathbf 1\{(x,w_j)\in B\}
K_j(dw_j\mid x,u)
\right]
Q_{a,j,z}(dx,du).
\end{aligned}
$$

The right-hand side is $(\mathcal K_jQ_{a,j,z})(B)$ by the definition of $\mathcal K_j$. Since this equality holds for every measurable set $B$,

$$
\mathcal L(X,W_j\mid A=a,Z_j=z)
=
\mathcal K_jQ_{a,j,z}.
$$

$\square$

### Definition 2: Exact cross-view balance

A representation $Z_j=\phi_j(X,W_{-j})$ satisfies exact cross-view balance if

$$
(X,W_j)\perp\!\!\!\perp A\mid Z_j.
\tag{EB}
$$

Under Assumption 4, this condition is equivalent to

$$
\mathcal L(X,W_j\mid A=1,Z_j=z)
=
\mathcal L(X,W_j\mid A=0,Z_j=z)
\tag{10}
$$

for every $z$ with $P(Z_j=z)>0$.

Exact cross-view balance is a population property of the observed distribution for a fixed representation. It is not an additional causal assumption. It can, in principle, be evaluated using the observed variables $(X,W_j,A,Z_j)$.

Combining equation (10) with Lemma 2 gives

$$
\boxed{
\mathcal K_jQ_{1,j,z}
=
\mathcal K_jQ_{0,j,z}.
}
\tag{11}
$$

Equation (11) states that the two latent treatment-arm distributions generate the same observed distribution of $(X,W_j)$ after passing through the held-out proxy channel.

### Theorem 2: Exact identification under cross-view balance

Suppose Assumptions 1 through 5 hold for a fixed mask $j$ and a fixed representation $Z_j=\phi_j(X,W_{-j})$. Suppose further that $Z_j$ satisfies exact cross-view balance:

$$
(X,W_j)\perp\!\!\!\perp A\mid Z_j.
$$

Then

$$
\boxed{
\tau_{Z_j}=\tau.
}
$$

Consequently, the average treatment effect is point identified by

$$
\boxed{
\tau
=
\sum_zP(Z_j=z)
\left[
\mathbb E(Y\mid A=1,Z_j=z)
-
\mathbb E(Y\mid A=0,Z_j=z)
\right].
}
\tag{ID}
$$

#### Proof

Fix any $z$ with $P(Z_j=z)>0$.

Exact cross-view balance gives

$$
\mathcal L(X,W_j\mid A=1,Z_j=z)
=
\mathcal L(X,W_j\mid A=0,Z_j=z).
$$

Lemma 2 factorizes the two distributions through the same held-out proxy channel. Therefore,

$$
\mathcal K_jQ_{1,j,z}
=
\mathcal K_jQ_{0,j,z}.
\tag{12}
$$

By construction,

$$
Q_{0,j,z},Q_{1,j,z}\in\mathcal Q_{j,z}.
$$

Assumption 5 can therefore be applied to the pair $(Q_{1,j,z},Q_{0,j,z})$. For each $a\in\{0,1\}$, equation (12) implies

$$
\mathbb E_{Q_{1,j,z}}\{\mu_{a,j,z}(X,U)\}
=
\mathbb E_{Q_{0,j,z}}\{\mu_{a,j,z}(X,U)\}.
\tag{13}
$$

Equation (13) makes both treatment-arm differences in the exact bias decomposition equal to zero. Hence,

$$
\tau_{Z_j}-\tau=0.
$$

Therefore,

$$
\tau_{Z_j}=\tau.
$$

The identification formula in equation (ID) follows from the definition of $\tau_{Z_j}$. $\square$

### Remark 1: What the theorem identifies

Theorem 2 identifies the average treatment effect through equality of outcome-relevant latent averages. It does not require

$$
Q_{1,j,z}=Q_{0,j,z}.
$$

It therefore does not require the full latent independence statement

$$
(X,U)\perp\!\!\!\perp A\mid Z_j.
$$

Assumption 5 only requires that two latent distributions producing the same held-out proxy distribution also produce the same conditional mean of each potential outcome. This is the precise role of outcome-relevant completeness.

### Remark 2: Exact balance and attainability

Theorem 2 applies to a representation that achieves exact cross-view balance. The theorem does not guarantee that such a representation exists in the chosen class $\Phi_j$.

A restricted representation class may contain no map that achieves exact cross-view balance. The next section defines a numerical measure of the remaining imbalance and studies how that imbalance is related to causal bias.

## Approximate Balance and Sensitivity

Throughout this section, the fixed representation remains $Z_j=\phi_j(X,W_{-j})$.

Theorem 2 considers the ideal case in which the two treatment arms have exactly the same conditional distribution of the held-out observed variables. Exact equality may be unavailable when the representation class is restricted.

We therefore consider the following quantitative question:

> If the two held-out distributions are close but not equal, how far can adjustment by $Z_j$ be from the true average treatment effect?

Answering this question requires two ingredients. First, we define an observed quantity that measures the remaining cross-view imbalance. Second, we impose a stability condition that connects this observed imbalance to the outcome-relevant latent differences appearing in Theorem 1.

### Definition 3: Cross-view discrepancy

For the fixed block $j$, define the held-out observed vector

$$
V_j
\triangleq
(X,W_j).
$$

Our purpose is to measure the difference between two probability distributions of $V_j$. A Hilbert space is a vector space equipped with an inner product and complete under its induced norm. Here we use a Hilbert space whose elements are functions. Its norm allows us to measure the distance between representations of probability distributions.

To construct these representations, let

$$
\kappa_j:
(\mathcal X\times\mathcal W_j)
\times
(\mathcal X\times\mathcal W_j)
\longrightarrow
\mathbb R
$$

be a bounded, measurable, positive-semidefinite kernel.

The value $\kappa_j(v,v')$ measures the similarity between two possible values $v$ and $v'$ of the held-out observed vector. The kernel determines a Hilbert space of functions denoted by $\mathcal H_j$, called its reproducing kernel Hilbert space.

For any probability distribution $P$ of $V_j$, define its kernel mean embedding by

$$
m_j(P)
\triangleq
\int \kappa_j(v,\cdot)\,P(dv)
\in\mathcal H_j.
$$

Boundedness of $\kappa_j$ guarantees that this integral is well-defined. The embedding $m_j(P)$ is a representation of the distribution $P$ as one element of $\mathcal H_j$.

For two probability distributions $P$ and $P'$ of $V_j$, define their maximum mean discrepancy by

$$
\operatorname{MMD}_{\kappa_j}(P,P')
\triangleq
\left\|
m_j(P)-m_j(P')
\right\|_{\mathcal H_j}.
$$

The maximum mean discrepancy is a nonnegative number. A larger value indicates a larger difference between the two distributions.

We assume that $\kappa_j$ is characteristic. This means that the kernel mean embedding uniquely determines the underlying distribution:

$$
\operatorname{MMD}_{\kappa_j}(P,P')=0
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
P=P'.
$$

For each retained representation value $z$, define the stratum-specific cross-view discrepancy

$$
\Delta_{j,z}(\phi_j)
\triangleq
\operatorname{MMD}_{\kappa_j}
\left\{
\mathcal L(V_j\mid A=1,Z_j=z),
\mathcal L(V_j\mid A=0,Z_j=z)
\right\}.
\tag{14}
$$

Thus, $\Delta_{j,z}(\phi_j)$ compares the distributions of the held-out observed vector $(X,W_j)$ between the treated and untreated units whose representation equals $z$.

Recall that

$$
p_{j,z}
=
P(Z_j=z).
$$

We define the aggregate cross-view discrepancy of $\phi_j$ by

$$
D_j(\phi_j)
\triangleq
\sum_z
p_{j,z}\Delta_{j,z}(\phi_j),
\tag{15}
$$

where the sum is taken over all retained values $z$ satisfying $p_{j,z}>0$.

The quantity $D_j(\phi_j)$ is a population functional of the observed distribution. It depends only on the observed variables $(X,W_j,A,Z_j)$.

### Proposition 1: Zero discrepancy is equivalent to exact cross-view balance

Under Assumption 4 and the characteristic-kernel condition,

$$
\boxed{
D_j(\phi_j)=0
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
(X,W_j)\perp\!\!\!\perp A\mid Z_j.
}
\tag{16}
$$

#### Proof

Each term in equation (15) is nonnegative. Moreover, every retained representation value satisfies

$$
p_{j,z}>0.
$$

Therefore,

$$
D_j(\phi_j)=0
$$

if and only if

$$
\Delta_{j,z}(\phi_j)=0
$$

for every retained $z$.

Because $\kappa_j$ is characteristic,

$$
\Delta_{j,z}(\phi_j)=0
$$

if and only if

$$
\mathcal L(V_j\mid A=1,Z_j=z)
=
\mathcal L(V_j\mid A=0,Z_j=z).
$$

Since $V_j=(X,W_j)$, this equality is equivalent to

$$
\mathcal L(X,W_j\mid A=1,Z_j=z)
=
\mathcal L(X,W_j\mid A=0,Z_j=z).
$$

Under Assumption 4, both conditional distributions are well-defined for every retained $z$. Their equality for every retained $z$ is equivalent to

$$
(X,W_j)\perp\!\!\!\perp A\mid Z_j.
$$

$\square$

Proposition 1 shows that $D_j(\phi_j)$ is a numerical relaxation of exact cross-view balance. The condition $D_j(\phi_j)=0$ is exactly the balance condition used in Theorem 2.

Lemma 2 also gives, for every treatment arm $a$ and every retained $z$,

$$
\mathcal L(V_j\mid A=a,Z_j=z)
=
\mathcal K_jQ_{a,j,z}.
$$

Consequently,

$$
\boxed{
\Delta_{j,z}(\phi_j)
=
\operatorname{MMD}_{\kappa_j}
\left(
\mathcal K_jQ_{1,j,z},
\mathcal K_jQ_{0,j,z}
\right).
}
\tag{17}
$$

Equation (17) connects the observed cross-view discrepancy to the two latent treatment-arm distributions.

### Assumption 6: Uniform outcome-relevant stability

Assume that there exists a finite constant

$$
\Gamma_j<\infty
$$

such that, for every retained representation value $z$, every treatment level $a\in\{0,1\}$, and every pair $Q,Q'\in\mathcal Q_{j,z}$,

$$
\begin{aligned}
&
\left|
\int\mu_{a,j,z}(x,u)\,Q(dx,du)
-
\int\mu_{a,j,z}(x,u)\,Q'(dx,du)
\right|
\\
&\qquad\le
\Gamma_j
\operatorname{MMD}_{\kappa_j}
\left(
\mathcal K_jQ,
\mathcal K_jQ'
\right).
\end{aligned}
\tag{A6}
$$

The left-hand side measures how much the mean of the potential outcome $Y(a)$ changes between the two latent distributions $Q$ and $Q'$.

The right-hand side measures how different those same latent distributions appear through the held-out observed vector $(X,W_j)$.

The constant $\Gamma_j$ converts an observed proxy discrepancy into an upper bound on an outcome-relevant latent difference. A small $\Gamma_j$ means that an outcome-relevant change in the latent distribution produces a detectable change in the held-out observed distribution. A large $\Gamma_j$ means that an outcome-relevant latent change may be difficult to detect through $(X,W_j)$.

The inequality is required for every pair $Q,Q'\in\mathcal Q_{j,z}$. It is therefore a restriction on the declared causal model. It is not imposed only on the two realized treatment-arm distributions.

### Theorem 3: Approximate cross-view bias bound

Suppose Assumptions 1 through 4 and Assumption 6 hold for the fixed representation

$$
Z_j=\phi_j(X,W_{-j}).
$$

Then

$$
\boxed{
|\tau_{Z_j}-\tau|
\le
\Gamma_jD_j(\phi_j).
}
\tag{18}
$$

#### Proof

For each retained representation value $z$ and treatment level $a\in\{0,1\}$, define

$$
\delta_{a,j,z}
\triangleq
\int\mu_{a,j,z}\,dQ_{1,j,z}
-
\int\mu_{a,j,z}\,dQ_{0,j,z}.
\tag{19}
$$

The quantity $\delta_{a,j,z}$ is the difference between the two treatment arms in the conditional mean of the potential outcome $Y(a)$ within representation value $z$.

The exact bias decomposition in Theorem 1 gives

$$
\tau_{Z_j}-\tau
=
\sum_z
p_{j,z}
\left[
(1-\pi_{j,z})\delta_{1,j,z}
+
\pi_{j,z}\delta_{0,j,z}
\right].
\tag{20}
$$

Applying the triangle inequality to equation (20) gives

$$
\begin{aligned}
|\tau_{Z_j}-\tau|
\le
\sum_z
p_{j,z}
\Big[
&(1-\pi_{j,z})|\delta_{1,j,z}|
\\
&+
\pi_{j,z}|\delta_{0,j,z}|
\Big].
\end{aligned}
\tag{21}
$$

By construction,

$$
Q_{0,j,z},Q_{1,j,z}
\in
\mathcal Q_{j,z}.
$$

We may therefore apply Assumption 6 with

$$
Q=Q_{1,j,z},
\qquad
Q'=Q_{0,j,z}.
$$

For each $a\in\{0,1\}$,

$$
|\delta_{a,j,z}|
\le
\Gamma_j
\operatorname{MMD}_{\kappa_j}
\left(
\mathcal K_jQ_{1,j,z},
\mathcal K_jQ_{0,j,z}
\right).
\tag{22}
$$

Equation (17) identifies the maximum mean discrepancy on the right-hand side:

$$
\operatorname{MMD}_{\kappa_j}
\left(
\mathcal K_jQ_{1,j,z},
\mathcal K_jQ_{0,j,z}
\right)
=
\Delta_{j,z}(\phi_j).
$$

Hence,

$$
|\delta_{a,j,z}|
\le
\Gamma_j\Delta_{j,z}(\phi_j).
\tag{23}
$$

Substituting equation (23) into equation (21) gives

$$
\begin{aligned}
|\tau_{Z_j}-\tau|
&\le
\sum_z
p_{j,z}
\Big[
(1-\pi_{j,z})
\Gamma_j\Delta_{j,z}(\phi_j)
+
\pi_{j,z}
\Gamma_j\Delta_{j,z}(\phi_j)
\Big]
\\
&=
\Gamma_j
\sum_z
p_{j,z}
\left[
(1-\pi_{j,z})+\pi_{j,z}
\right]
\Delta_{j,z}(\phi_j)
\\
&=
\Gamma_j
\sum_z
p_{j,z}
\Delta_{j,z}(\phi_j).
\end{aligned}
$$

By equation (15),

$$
\sum_z
p_{j,z}
\Delta_{j,z}(\phi_j)
=
D_j(\phi_j).
$$

Therefore,

$$
|\tau_{Z_j}-\tau|
\le
\Gamma_jD_j(\phi_j).
$$

$\square$

### Remark 3: Assumption 5 and Assumption 6 have different roles

Assumption 5 is an exact zero-preservation condition. It states that

$$
\mathcal K_jQ=\mathcal K_jQ'
$$

implies equality of the corresponding potential-outcome means.

Assumption 6 provides a quantitative relationship for distributions that are close but not necessarily equal. In particular, if

$$
\mathcal K_jQ=\mathcal K_jQ',
$$

then

$$
\operatorname{MMD}_{\kappa_j}
\left(
\mathcal K_jQ,
\mathcal K_jQ'
\right)
=
0.
$$

Assumption 6 then gives

$$
\int\mu_{a,j,z}\,dQ
=
\int\mu_{a,j,z}\,dQ'.
$$

Therefore, Assumption 6 implies Assumption 5.

The converse does not generally hold. Assumption 5 may be valid even when no finite constant $\Gamma_j$ satisfies Assumption 6.

Theorem 2 uses Assumption 5 because exact identification only requires equality at zero discrepancy. Theorem 3 uses the stronger Assumption 6 because approximate balance requires a numerical relationship between observed discrepancy and causal bias.

### Remark 4: Interpretation as a sensitivity analysis

The discrepancy $D_j(\phi_j)$ is determined by the observed population distribution. The stability budget $\Gamma_j$ depends on latent distributions and potential-outcome means. It is not generally identified from the observed distribution of $(X,W,A,Y)$.

For a scientifically specified value of $\Gamma_j$, Theorem 3 gives

$$
\boxed{
\tau
\in
\left[
\tau_{Z_j}-\Gamma_jD_j(\phi_j),
\;
\tau_{Z_j}+\Gamma_jD_j(\phi_j)
\right].
}
\tag{24}
$$

This interval measures uncertainty caused by residual hidden confounding under the specified stability budget. It is a population sensitivity interval. It does not yet account for sampling uncertainty from estimating $\tau_{Z_j}$ or $D_j(\phi_j)$ with finite data.

A small value of $D_j(\phi_j)$ alone does not guarantee a small causal bias. The causal sensitivity bound is the product

$$
\Gamma_jD_j(\phi_j).
$$

If the held-out proxy is weak in an outcome-relevant direction, $\Gamma_j$ may be very large or infinite. In that case, a small observed discrepancy provides little information about the remaining causal bias.

### Remark 5: Uniform stability across candidate representations

Theorem 3 concerns one fixed representation $\phi_j$. A representation-learning procedure will compare multiple maps in $\Phi_j$.

For each candidate map $\phi\in\Phi_j$, let $\Gamma_j(\phi)$ denote a stability budget satisfying Assumption 6 for that map. To justify learning by minimizing $D_j(\phi)$, assume that one finite constant $\overline{\Gamma}_j$ satisfies

$$
\sup_{\phi\in\Phi_j}
\Gamma_j(\phi)
\le
\overline{\Gamma}_j
<
\infty.
\tag{25}
$$

This condition requires the stability bound to hold uniformly over the representation class considered by the learner.

Under equation (25), every candidate representation satisfies

$$
|\tau_{Z_j}-\tau|
\le
\overline{\Gamma}_jD_j(\phi_j).
$$

Consequently, a representation with a smaller value of $D_j(\phi_j)$ has a smaller certified upper bound on its causal bias.

Without equation (25), a learner could select a representation with a small observed discrepancy and an arbitrarily large stability budget. Minimizing the observed discrepancy would then fail to control the causal bias.

Theorem 3 therefore provides the causal foundation for representation learning. The next step is to define how a representation is selected by minimizing the cross-view discrepancy.

# Representation Learning and AIPW Estimation

The previous results concern one fixed representation. We now study a representation selected from data.

The analysis has two distinct targets. The representation learner seeks a map with small cross-view discrepancy. The causal estimator then estimates the adjustment functional induced by the selected map. An honest sample split keeps these two statistical problems separate.

## Honest sample splitting

Partition the observed sample into three mutually disjoint subsets:

$$
\mathcal I_{\mathrm D},
\qquad
\mathcal I_{\mathrm N},
\qquad
\mathcal I_{\mathrm E}.
\tag{SPLIT}
$$

Their respective sample sizes are denoted by

$$
n_{\mathrm D},
\qquad
n_{\mathrm N},
\qquad
n_{\mathrm E}.
$$

The three subsets have different roles.

1. The discrepancy-learning sample $\mathcal I_{\mathrm D}$ is used to calculate empirical cross-view discrepancies and select a representation.
2. The nuisance-learning sample $\mathcal I_{\mathrm N}$ is used to estimate the conditional outcome means and treatment propensity required by the AIPW estimator.
3. The evaluation sample $\mathcal I_{\mathrm E}$ is used to calculate the final causal estimate.

Because the original observations are independent, the three subsets are independent. Conditional on $\mathcal I_{\mathrm D}$, the selected representation is fixed when the nuisance functions are estimated. Conditional on both $\mathcal I_{\mathrm D}$ and $\mathcal I_{\mathrm N}$, the selected representation and estimated nuisance functions are fixed when the AIPW estimator is evaluated.

## A finite candidate library

The representation class $\Phi_j$ introduced above may be large or infinite. For the finite-sample learning result, fix a finite candidate library

$$
\mathcal F_j\subseteq\Phi_j,
\qquad
N_j\triangleq|\mathcal F_j|<\infty.
$$

For each candidate map $\phi\in\mathcal F_j$, define

$$
Z_j^\phi
\triangleq
\phi(X,W_{-j}).
$$

Let

$$
\mathcal R_j(\phi)
\triangleq
\left\{
z\in\{1,\ldots,M_j\}:
P(Z_j^\phi=z)>0
\right\}
$$

denote the set of positive-mass strata generated by $\phi$.

For the learning result, assume that there exist constants

$$
p_{\min}>0,
\qquad
\eta\in(0,1/2]
$$

such that every candidate map $\phi\in\mathcal F_j$ and every $z\in\mathcal R_j(\phi)$ satisfy

$$
P(Z_j^\phi=z)\ge p_{\min}
\tag{LM}
$$

and

$$
\eta
\le
P(A=1\mid Z_j^\phi=z)
\le
1-\eta.
\tag{LO}
$$

Condition (LM) prevents the candidate library from producing arbitrarily small population strata. Condition (LO) requires population overlap uniformly across the candidate library.

The causal assumptions must also be interpreted uniformly over the candidate library.

Assumptions 2 and 3 are primitive conditions for the fixed held-out block $j$. They do not depend on which candidate map is selected. Because every $Z_j^\phi$ is a measurable function of $(X,W_{-j})$, the conditioning-safety and cross-view factorization arguments apply to every $\phi\in\mathcal F_j$.

Assumption 4 is representation-specific. It must hold for every $Z_j^\phi$ generated by a candidate map. Conditions (LM) and (LO) impose a uniform version of this requirement.

Assumption 6 is also representation-specific because the strata, latent conditional distributions, potential-outcome means, and stability budget can change with $\phi$. For every $\phi\in\mathcal F_j$, Assumption 6 must hold for the corresponding candidate-specific objects. In addition, equation (25) requires their stability budgets to be bounded by the same finite constant $\overline\Gamma_j$.

Finally, because the kernel $\kappa_j$ is bounded, fix a finite constant $\rho_j$ satisfying

$$
\sup_v\sqrt{\kappa_j(v,v)}
\le
\rho_j.
\tag{KB}
$$

The norm of every kernel mean embedding is then at most $\rho_j$, and every stratum-specific maximum mean discrepancy is at most $2\rho_j$.

### Definition 4: Empirical cross-view discrepancy

For an observation $i\in\mathcal I_{\mathrm D}$ and a candidate map $\phi\in\mathcal F_j$, write

$$
Z_{j,i}^{\phi}
\triangleq
\phi(X_i,W_{-j,i})
$$

and

$$
V_{j,i}
\triangleq
(X_i,W_{j,i}).
$$

For each treatment arm $a\in\{0,1\}$ and stratum $z\in\{1,\ldots,M_j\}$, define the cell count

$$
N_{a,z}^{\phi}
\triangleq
\sum_{i\in\mathcal I_{\mathrm D}}
\mathbf 1\{A_i=a,Z_{j,i}^{\phi}=z\}.
\tag{CELL}
$$

Define the total stratum count and empirical stratum mass by

$$
N_z^\phi
\triangleq
N_{0,z}^{\phi}+N_{1,z}^{\phi},
\qquad
\widehat p_{j,z}(\phi)
\triangleq
\frac{N_z^\phi}{n_{\mathrm D}}.
$$

When $N_{a,z}^{\phi}>0$, define the empirical kernel mean embedding in treatment arm $a$ and stratum $z$ by

$$
\widehat m_{a,j,z}(\phi)
\triangleq
\frac{1}{N_{a,z}^{\phi}}
\sum_{i\in\mathcal I_{\mathrm D}}
\mathbf 1\{A_i=a,Z_{j,i}^{\phi}=z\}
\kappa_j(V_{j,i},\cdot).
$$

When both treatment-arm cells are nonempty, define

$$
\widehat\Delta_{j,z}(\phi)
\triangleq
\left\|
\widehat m_{1,j,z}(\phi)
-
\widehat m_{0,j,z}(\phi)
\right\|_{\mathcal H_j}.
$$

The empirical cross-view discrepancy is

$$
\widehat D_j(\phi)
\triangleq
\sum_{z:N_z^\phi>0}
\widehat p_{j,z}(\phi)
\widehat\Delta_{j,z}(\phi).
\tag{ED}
$$

If an occupied empirical stratum has observations from only one treatment arm, set

$$
\widehat D_j(\phi)=+\infty.
$$

This convention prevents a candidate with an empirically unsupported treatment comparison from being selected solely because its discrepancy cannot be calculated. Under conditions (LM) and (LO), all population-positive treatment-arm cells are nonempty with high probability as $n_{\mathrm D}$ increases.

### Proposition 2: Uniform deviation over the finite candidate library

Let $\delta_{\mathrm D}\in(0,1)$ and suppose

$$
n_{\mathrm D}
\ge
\frac{8}{p_{\min}\eta}
\log
\left(
\frac{8M_jN_j}{\delta_{\mathrm D}}
\right).
\tag{LS}
$$

Define

$$
e_{j,n_{\mathrm D}}(\delta_{\mathrm D})
\triangleq
\rho_j
\left[
2+
\sqrt{
2\log
\left(
\frac{4M_jN_j}{\delta_{\mathrm D}}
\right)
}
\right]
\sqrt{
\frac{2}{n_{\mathrm D}p_{\min}\eta}
}
$$

and

$$
\begin{aligned}
r_{j,n_{\mathrm D}}(\delta_{\mathrm D})
\triangleq{}&
2e_{j,n_{\mathrm D}}(\delta_{\mathrm D})
\\
&+
2\rho_j
\sqrt{
\frac{
2\left[
M_j\log 2+
\log\left(
\frac{4N_j}{\delta_{\mathrm D}}
\right)
\right]
}{
n_{\mathrm D}
}
}.
\end{aligned}
\tag{RAD}
$$

Under conditions (LM), (LO), and (KB), with probability at least $1-\delta_{\mathrm D}$,

$$
\boxed{
\sup_{\phi\in\mathcal F_j}
\left|
\widehat D_j(\phi)-D_j(\phi)
\right|
\le
r_{j,n_{\mathrm D}}(\delta_{\mathrm D}).
}
\tag{UD}
$$

#### Proof

Fix a candidate map $\phi$ and a positive-mass stratum $z$.

Conditions (LM) and (LO) imply

$$
P(A=a,Z_j^\phi=z)
\ge
p_{\min}\eta
$$

for both treatment arms. A multiplicative Chernoff inequality gives

$$
P\left(
N_{a,z}^{\phi}
<
\frac{n_{\mathrm D}p_{\min}\eta}{2}
\right)
\le
\exp
\left(
-\frac{n_{\mathrm D}p_{\min}\eta}{8}
\right).
$$

There are at most $2M_jN_j$ candidate treatment-arm cells. Condition (LS) and a union bound therefore imply that, with failure probability at most $\delta_{\mathrm D}/4$,

$$
N_{a,z}^{\phi}
\ge
\frac{n_{\mathrm D}p_{\min}\eta}{2}
\tag{CE}
$$

simultaneously for every candidate map, positive-mass stratum, and treatment arm.

Conditional on the cell assignments for one fixed candidate map, the observations in each cell are independent draws from the corresponding conditional distribution of $V_j$. Hilbert-space mean concentration and equation (CE) imply that, with total failure probability at most $\delta_{\mathrm D}/2$,

$$
\left\|
\widehat m_{a,j,z}(\phi)
-
m_{a,j,z}(\phi)
\right\|_{\mathcal H_j}
\le
e_{j,n_{\mathrm D}}(\delta_{\mathrm D})
$$

simultaneously over all candidate treatment-arm cells. The reverse triangle inequality then gives

$$
\left|
\widehat\Delta_{j,z}(\phi)
-
\Delta_{j,z}(\phi)
\right|
\le
2e_{j,n_{\mathrm D}}(\delta_{\mathrm D}).
\tag{DE}
$$

For each fixed candidate $\phi$, the vector of empirical stratum masses is a multinomial empirical distribution. A multinomial $L_1$ concentration inequality and a union bound over the $N_j$ candidates imply that, with total failure probability at most $\delta_{\mathrm D}/4$,

$$
\sum_z
\left|
\widehat p_{j,z}(\phi)
-
p_{j,z}(\phi)
\right|
\le
\sqrt{
\frac{
2\left[
M_j\log 2+
\log\left(
\frac{4N_j}{\delta_{\mathrm D}}
\right)
\right]
}{
n_{\mathrm D}
}
}
\tag{WE}
$$

simultaneously over $\phi\in\mathcal F_j$.

On the intersection of events (CE), (DE), and (WE),

$$
\begin{aligned}
&
\left|
\widehat D_j(\phi)-D_j(\phi)
\right|
\\
&\le
\sum_z
\widehat p_{j,z}(\phi)
\left|
\widehat\Delta_{j,z}(\phi)
-
\Delta_{j,z}(\phi)
\right|
+
\sum_z
\left|
\widehat p_{j,z}(\phi)
-
p_{j,z}(\phi)
\right|
\Delta_{j,z}(\phi)
\\
&\le
2e_{j,n_{\mathrm D}}(\delta_{\mathrm D})
+
2\rho_j
\sum_z
\left|
\widehat p_{j,z}(\phi)
-
p_{j,z}(\phi)
\right|
\\
&\le
r_{j,n_{\mathrm D}}(\delta_{\mathrm D}).
\end{aligned}
$$

The first term uses equation (DE) and the fact that the empirical stratum masses sum to one. The second term uses

$$
0\le\Delta_{j,z}(\phi)\le2\rho_j.
$$

The three failure probabilities sum to $\delta_{\mathrm D}$. This proves equation (UD). $\square$

### Definition 5: Approximate empirical discrepancy minimizer

Let $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{opt},j}\ge0$ denote an optimization tolerance. The representation-learning algorithm returns a map

$$
\widehat\phi_j\in\mathcal F_j
$$

satisfying

$$
\widehat D_j(\widehat\phi_j)
\le
\inf_{\phi\in\mathcal F_j}
\widehat D_j(\phi)
+
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{opt},j}.
\tag{ERM}
$$

The selected representation is

$$
\widehat Z_j
\triangleq
\widehat\phi_j(X,W_{-j}).
$$

The tolerance $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{opt},j}$ separates statistical error from optimization error. It is zero for an exact empirical minimizer.

### Theorem 4: Representation-learning oracle inequality

Suppose the conditions of Proposition 2 hold. Suppose Assumptions 1, 2, and 3 hold for the fixed held-out block $j$. Suppose Assumptions 4 and 6 hold for every candidate representation $Z_j^\phi$ with $\phi\in\mathcal F_j$. Suppose also that the candidate-specific stability budgets satisfy equation (25) with the common finite bound $\overline\Gamma_j$.

Then, with probability at least $1-\delta_{\mathrm D}$,

$$
\boxed{
D_j(\widehat\phi_j)
\le
\inf_{\phi\in\mathcal F_j}
D_j(\phi)
+
2r_{j,n_{\mathrm D}}(\delta_{\mathrm D})
+
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{opt},j}.
}
\tag{OI-D}
$$

On the same event,

$$
\boxed{
\left|
\tau_{\widehat Z_j}-\tau
\right|
\le
\overline\Gamma_j
\left[
\inf_{\phi\in\mathcal F_j}
D_j(\phi)
+
2r_{j,n_{\mathrm D}}(\delta_{\mathrm D})
+
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{opt},j}
\right].
}
\tag{OI-C}
$$

#### Proof

On the event (UD), every $\phi\in\mathcal F_j$ satisfies

$$
D_j(\phi)
\le
\widehat D_j(\phi)
+
r_{j,n_{\mathrm D}}(\delta_{\mathrm D})
$$

and

$$
\widehat D_j(\phi)
\le
D_j(\phi)
+
r_{j,n_{\mathrm D}}(\delta_{\mathrm D}).
$$

Applying the first inequality to $\widehat\phi_j$, using equation (ERM), and then applying the second inequality to every candidate gives

$$
\begin{aligned}
D_j(\widehat\phi_j)
&\le
\widehat D_j(\widehat\phi_j)
+
r_{j,n_{\mathrm D}}(\delta_{\mathrm D})
\\
&\le
\inf_{\phi\in\mathcal F_j}
\widehat D_j(\phi)
+
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{opt},j}
+
r_{j,n_{\mathrm D}}(\delta_{\mathrm D})
\\
&\le
\inf_{\phi\in\mathcal F_j}
D_j(\phi)
+
2r_{j,n_{\mathrm D}}(\delta_{\mathrm D})
+
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{opt},j}.
\end{aligned}
$$

This proves equation (OI-D).

Conditional on the discrepancy-learning sample, $\widehat\phi_j$ is one fixed element of $\mathcal F_j$. Theorem 3 and uniform stability give

$$
\left|
\tau_{\widehat Z_j}-\tau
\right|
\le
\overline\Gamma_jD_j(\widehat\phi_j).
$$

Substituting equation (OI-D) proves equation (OI-C). $\square$

Theorem 4 controls the causal bias of the population adjustment functional induced by the learned representation. The functional $\tau_{\widehat Z_j}$ is not directly observed because it must be estimated from finite data. We next estimate it with an augmented inverse probability weighted estimator.

## AIPW estimation after representation learning

Conditional on the discrepancy-learning sample, the selected map $\widehat\phi_j$ is fixed. For a fresh observation, define

$$
\widehat Z_j
=
\widehat\phi_j(X,W_{-j}).
$$

For $a\in\{0,1\}$ and each value $z$ of $\widehat Z_j$, define the conditional outcome mean

$$
m_{a,j}(z)
\triangleq
\mathbb E(Y\mid A=a,\widehat Z_j=z)
$$

and the representation propensity

$$
e_j(z)
\triangleq
P(A=1\mid\widehat Z_j=z).
\tag{NUIS}
$$

These are nuisance functions. They are needed to estimate the adjustment functional $\tau_{\widehat Z_j}$, but they are not themselves the causal target.

For a fixed representation, the efficient influence function of the observed adjustment functional $\tau_{\widehat Z_j}$ in the nonparametric model for $(\widehat Z_j,A,Y)$ is

$$
\begin{aligned}
\psi_j(O)
\triangleq{}&
m_{1,j}(\widehat Z_j)
-
m_{0,j}(\widehat Z_j)
\\
&+
\frac{
A\{Y-m_{1,j}(\widehat Z_j)\}
}{
e_j(\widehat Z_j)
}
-
\frac{
(1-A)\{Y-m_{0,j}(\widehat Z_j)\}
}{
1-e_j(\widehat Z_j)
}
-
\tau_{\widehat Z_j}.
\end{aligned}
\tag{EIF}
$$

The function $\psi_j$ has mean zero. It describes the first-order sampling variation of regular estimators of $\tau_{\widehat Z_j}$ when the representation is treated as fixed.

Use the nuisance-learning sample $\mathcal I_{\mathrm N}$ to estimate

$$
m_{0,j},
\qquad
m_{1,j},
\qquad
e_j.
$$

Denote the resulting estimators by

$$
\widehat m_{0,j},
\qquad
\widehat m_{1,j},
\qquad
\widehat e_j.
$$

### Definition 6: Honest AIPW estimator

For an evaluation observation $i\in\mathcal I_{\mathrm E}$, define

$$
\widehat Z_{j,i}
\triangleq
\widehat\phi_j(X_i,W_{-j,i}).
$$

The AIPW score is

$$
\begin{aligned}
S_j(O_i)
\triangleq{}&
\widehat m_{1,j}(\widehat Z_{j,i})
-
\widehat m_{0,j}(\widehat Z_{j,i})
\\
&+
\frac{
A_i\{Y_i-\widehat m_{1,j}(\widehat Z_{j,i})\}
}{
\widehat e_j(\widehat Z_{j,i})
}
\\
&-
\frac{
(1-A_i)\{Y_i-\widehat m_{0,j}(\widehat Z_{j,i})\}
}{
1-\widehat e_j(\widehat Z_{j,i})
}.
\end{aligned}
\tag{AS}
$$

The honest AIPW estimator is

$$
\boxed{
\widehat\tau_j^{\mathrm{AIPW}}
\triangleq
\frac{1}{n_{\mathrm E}}
\sum_{i\in\mathcal I_{\mathrm E}}
S_j(O_i).
}
\tag{AIPW}
$$

The adjective *honest* refers to the sample separation. The observations used in the final average were not used to learn the representation or the nuisance functions.

### Proposition 3: Exact signed nuisance remainder

Conditional on the discrepancy-learning and nuisance-learning samples,

$$
\begin{aligned}
&
\mathbb E\{S_j(O)\}
-
\tau_{\widehat Z_j}
\\
&=
\mathbb E
\Bigg[
\left\{
\widehat e_j(\widehat Z_j)
-
e_j(\widehat Z_j)
\right\}
\Bigg\{
\frac{
\widehat m_{1,j}(\widehat Z_j)
-
m_{1,j}(\widehat Z_j)
}{
\widehat e_j(\widehat Z_j)
}
\\
&\hspace{47mm}
+
\frac{
\widehat m_{0,j}(\widehat Z_j)
-
m_{0,j}(\widehat Z_j)
}{
1-\widehat e_j(\widehat Z_j)
}
\Bigg\}
\Bigg].
\end{aligned}
\tag{REM}
$$

The expectation is over a fresh observation drawn independently of the two learning samples.

#### Proof

Fix a value $z$ of $\widehat Z_j$. Conditional on $\widehat Z_j=z$,

$$
\mathbb E(A\mid\widehat Z_j=z)
=
e_j(z)
$$

and

$$
\mathbb E(Y\mid A=a,\widehat Z_j=z)
=
m_{a,j}(z).
$$

Taking the conditional expectation of the AIPW score gives

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbb E\{S_j(O)\mid\widehat Z_j=z\}
={}&
\widehat m_{1,j}(z)-\widehat m_{0,j}(z)
\\
&+
\frac{
e_j(z)\{m_{1,j}(z)-\widehat m_{1,j}(z)\}
}{
\widehat e_j(z)
}
\\
&-
\frac{
\{1-e_j(z)\}\{m_{0,j}(z)-\widehat m_{0,j}(z)\}
}{
1-\widehat e_j(z)
}.
\end{aligned}
$$

Collecting the coefficients of the two nuisance-estimation errors gives

$$
\begin{aligned}
&
\mathbb E\{S_j(O)\mid\widehat Z_j=z\}
-
\{m_{1,j}(z)-m_{0,j}(z)\}
\\
&=
\{\widehat e_j(z)-e_j(z)\}
\left[
\frac{
\widehat m_{1,j}(z)-m_{1,j}(z)
}{
\widehat e_j(z)
}
+
\frac{
\widehat m_{0,j}(z)-m_{0,j}(z)
}{
1-\widehat e_j(z)
}
\right].
\end{aligned}
$$

Averaging over $\widehat Z_j$ proves equation (REM). $\square$

Equation (REM) is the source of double robustness. The signed remainder is zero if the propensity estimator is correct. It is also zero if both conditional outcome estimators are correct.

### Theorem 5: Conditional finite-sample AIPW error

Suppose

$$
|Y|\le B
\qquad\text{almost surely}
\tag{YB}
$$

for some finite $B$. Clip the nuisance estimates so that

$$
-B
\le
\widehat m_{a,j}(z)
\le
B
$$

and

$$
\eta
\le
\widehat e_j(z)
\le
1-\eta
\tag{NC}
$$

for every treatment arm and representation value.

For a function $g$ of $\widehat Z_j$, define

$$
\|g\|_{2,\widehat Z_j}
\triangleq
\left[
\mathbb E\{g(\widehat Z_j)^2
\mid
\mathcal I_{\mathrm D},\mathcal I_{\mathrm N}\}
\right]^{1/2},
$$

where the conditional expectation is over a fresh observation.

After the discrepancy-learning and nuisance-learning samples have been observed, define the realized conditional nuisance errors

$$
q_{e,j}
\triangleq
\left\|
\widehat e_j-e_j
\right\|_{2,\widehat Z_j}
$$

and

$$
q_{a,j}
\triangleq
\left\|
\widehat m_{a,j}-m_{a,j}
\right\|_{2,\widehat Z_j},
\qquad
a\in\{0,1\}.
\tag{NE}
$$

These quantities are theoretical errors relative to the true nuisance functions. They are not generally observable from the data.

For $\delta_{\mathrm E}\in(0,1)$, define

$$
\begin{aligned}
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{AIPW},j}
\triangleq{}&
2B\left(1+\frac{1}{\eta}\right)
\sqrt{
\frac{
2\log(2/\delta_{\mathrm E})
}{
n_{\mathrm E}
}
}
\\
&+
\frac{
q_{e,j}(q_{0,j}+q_{1,j})
}{
\eta
}.
\end{aligned}
\tag{AE}
$$

Conditional on $\mathcal I_{\mathrm D}$ and $\mathcal I_{\mathrm N}$, with probability at least $1-\delta_{\mathrm E}$ over the evaluation sample,

$$
\boxed{
\left|
\widehat\tau_j^{\mathrm{AIPW}}
-
\tau_{\widehat Z_j}
\right|
\le
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{AIPW},j}.
}
\tag{AEB}
$$

#### Proof

The clipping conditions and equation (YB) imply

$$
|S_j(O)|
\le
2B+\frac{2B}{\eta}
=
2B\left(1+\frac{1}{\eta}\right).
$$

Conditional on $\mathcal I_{\mathrm D}$ and $\mathcal I_{\mathrm N}$, the evaluation scores are independent and identically distributed. Hoeffding's inequality therefore gives, with probability at least $1-\delta_{\mathrm E}$,

$$
\left|
\widehat\tau_j^{\mathrm{AIPW}}
-
\mathbb E\{S_j(O)
\mid
\mathcal I_{\mathrm D},\mathcal I_{\mathrm N}\}
\right|
\le
2B\left(1+\frac{1}{\eta}\right)
\sqrt{
\frac{
2\log(2/\delta_{\mathrm E})
}{
n_{\mathrm E}
}
}.
\tag{SE}
$$

Proposition 3 and the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality give

$$
\begin{align}
&
\left|
\mathbb E\{S_j(O)
\mid
\mathcal I_{\mathrm D},\mathcal I_{\mathrm N}\}
-
\tau_{\widehat Z_j}
\right| \le
\frac{1}{\eta}
\left\|
\widehat e_j-e_j
\right\|_{2,\widehat Z_j}
\left[
\left\|
\widehat m_{0,j}-m_{0,j}
\right\|_{2,\widehat Z_j}
+
\left\|
\widehat m_{1,j}-m_{1,j}
\right\|_{2,\widehat Z_j}
\right] =
\frac{
q_{e,j}(q_{0,j}+q_{1,j})
}{
\eta
}.
\tag{NR}
\end{align}
$$

Combining equations (SE) and (NR) proves equation (AEB). $\square$

### Theorem 6: Conditional end-to-end sensitivity bound after representation learning

Suppose the conditions of Proposition 2, Theorem 4, and Theorem 5 hold. In particular, suppose the representation-specific stability budgets are uniformly bounded by the declared constant $\overline\Gamma_j$.

Then, jointly over the three independent samples $\mathcal I_{\mathrm D}$, $\mathcal I_{\mathrm N}$, and $\mathcal I_{\mathrm E}$, with probability at least

$$
1-\delta_{\mathrm D}-\delta_{\mathrm E},
$$

the learned-representation AIPW estimator satisfies

$$
\boxed{
\begin{aligned}
\left|
\widehat\tau_j^{\mathrm{AIPW}}-\tau
\right|
\le{}&
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{AIPW},j}
\\
&+
\overline\Gamma_j
\left[
\widehat D_j(\widehat\phi_j)
+
r_{j,n_{\mathrm D}}(\delta_{\mathrm D})
\right].
\end{aligned}
}
\tag{E2E-D}
$$

It also satisfies the oracle form

$$
\boxed{
\begin{aligned}
\left|
\widehat\tau_j^{\mathrm{AIPW}}-\tau
\right|
\le{}&
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{AIPW},j}
\\
&+
\overline\Gamma_j
\left[
\inf_{\phi\in\mathcal F_j}
D_j(\phi)
+
2r_{j,n_{\mathrm D}}(\delta_{\mathrm D})
+
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{opt},j}
\right].
\end{aligned}
}
\tag{E2E-O}
$$

The nuisance-learning sample enters through the realized conditional errors $q_{e,j}$, $q_{0,j}$, and $q_{1,j}$.

#### Proof

The triangle inequality gives

$$
\left|
\widehat\tau_j^{\mathrm{AIPW}}-\tau
\right|
\le
\left|
\widehat\tau_j^{\mathrm{AIPW}}
-
\tau_{\widehat Z_j}
\right|
+
\left|
\tau_{\widehat Z_j}-\tau
\right|.
\tag{E2E-1}
$$

Conditional on $\mathcal I_{\mathrm D}$ and $\mathcal I_{\mathrm N}$, Theorem 5 bounds the first term by $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{AIPW},j}$ with evaluation-sample failure probability at most $\delta_{\mathrm E}$.

Theorem 3 and uniform stability bound the second term by

$$
\left|
\tau_{\widehat Z_j}-\tau
\right|
\le
\overline\Gamma_jD_j(\widehat\phi_j).
\tag{E2E-2}
$$

On the uniform deviation event, whose failure probability over $\mathcal I_{\mathrm D}$ is at most $\delta_{\mathrm D}$,

$$
D_j(\widehat\phi_j)
\le
\widehat D_j(\widehat\phi_j)
+
r_{j,n_{\mathrm D}}(\delta_{\mathrm D}).
$$

Substituting this inequality into equations (E2E-1) and (E2E-2) proves equation (E2E-D).

Theorem 4 also gives

$$
D_j(\widehat\phi_j)
\le
\inf_{\phi\in\mathcal F_j}
D_j(\phi)
+
2r_{j,n_{\mathrm D}}(\delta_{\mathrm D})
+
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{opt},j}.
$$

Substituting this inequality proves equation (E2E-O).

The uniform deviation event fails with probability at most $\delta_{\mathrm D}$ over $\mathcal I_{\mathrm D}$. For every realization of $\mathcal I_{\mathrm D}$ and $\mathcal I_{\mathrm N}$, Theorem 5 gives a conditional evaluation-sample failure probability of at most $\delta_{\mathrm E}$. Integrating this conditional guarantee over the joint distribution of $\mathcal I_{\mathrm D}$ and $\mathcal I_{\mathrm N}$ preserves the failure probability $\delta_{\mathrm E}$. A union bound therefore gives joint probability at least $1-\delta_{\mathrm D}-\delta_{\mathrm E}$ over all three samples.

The realized nuisance-error quantities $q_{e,j}$, $q_{0,j}$, and $q_{1,j}$ remain random because they depend on $\mathcal I_{\mathrm D}$ and $\mathcal I_{\mathrm N}$. The inequalities hold with these realized quantities on their right-hand sides. $\square$

### Corollary 1: Consistency of the learned-representation AIPW estimator

Consider a sequence of candidate libraries $\mathcal F_{j,n}$, sample splits, nuisance estimators, optimization tolerances, and stability budgets satisfying the conditions of Theorem 6.

Let the confidence parameters satisfy

$$
\delta_{\mathrm D,n}
+
\delta_{\mathrm E,n}
\longrightarrow
0.
\tag{C1}
$$

Suppose the evaluation-sample term vanishes:

$$
\frac{
\log(2/\delta_{\mathrm E,n})
}{
n_{\mathrm E}
}
\longrightarrow
0.
\tag{C2}
$$

Suppose the realized nuisance-product remainder satisfies

$$
q_{e,j,n}
\left(
q_{0,j,n}+q_{1,j,n}
\right)
\xrightarrow{p}
0.
\tag{C3}
$$

Finally, suppose the stability-weighted representation error satisfies

$$
\overline\Gamma_{j,n}
\left[
\inf_{\phi\in\mathcal F_{j,n}}
D_j(\phi)
+
2r_{j,n_{\mathrm D}}(\delta_{\mathrm D,n})
+
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{opt},j,n}
\right]
\xrightarrow{p}
0.
\tag{C4}
$$

Then

$$
\boxed{
\widehat\tau_{j,n}^{\mathrm{AIPW}}
\xrightarrow{p}
\tau.
}
$$

#### Proof

By condition (C2), the evaluation-sample component of $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{AIPW},j}$ converges to zero. By condition (C3), its nuisance-product component converges to zero in probability. Condition (C4) makes the stability-weighted representation term in equation (E2E-O) converge to zero in probability.

Theorem 6 holds outside an event whose probability is at most

$$
\delta_{\mathrm D,n}
+
\delta_{\mathrm E,n}.
$$

This probability converges to zero by condition (C1). Therefore, every term in the end-to-end bound converges to zero in probability, which implies

$$
\widehat\tau_{j,n}^{\mathrm{AIPW}}
\xrightarrow{p}
\tau.
$$

$\square$

### Remark 6: Interpretation and observability

Theorem 6 decomposes the final causal error into

$$
\left|
\widehat\tau_j^{\mathrm{AIPW}}-\tau
\right|
\le
\underbrace{
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{AIPW},j}
}_{\text{AIPW estimation term}}
+
\underbrace{
\overline\Gamma_j
\left[
\widehat D_j(\widehat\phi_j)
+
r_{j,n_{\mathrm D}}(\delta_{\mathrm D})
\right]
}_{\text{hidden-confounding sensitivity term}}.
$$

This is a finite-sample sensitivity bound indexed by two kinds of unknown budgets.

First, $\overline\Gamma_j$ measures outcome-relevant stability of the held-out proxy channel. It is not generally identified from the observed distribution.

Second, $q_{e,j}$, $q_{0,j}$, and $q_{1,j}$ are errors relative to the true nuisance functions. They appear inside $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{AIPW},j}$ and are not directly observed.

Therefore, equation (E2E-D) is not, by itself, a fully observable confidence bound. It becomes operational after the stability and nuisance-error budgets are bounded by additional theory, external information, or a specified nuisance-estimation analysis. Its present role is to separate representation error, hidden-confounding sensitivity, nuisance error, and evaluation-sample variation without combining them into one unexplained remainder.

### Remark 7: Scope of the learning theorem

The finite-class result applies to hard, finite-valued representations in the prespecified library $\mathcal F_j$. It does not establish a generalization bound for neural networks, soft assignments, or a continuous representation.

If a practical algorithm learns a soft representation and later converts it into hard strata, an additional approximation term is required to control the difference between the optimized soft objective and the final hard-strata discrepancy. That term is not included in Theorem 6.

The honest three-way split also differs from cross-fitting. Cross-fitting can recover efficiency lost by splitting the data, but it requires an additional fold-by-fold analysis. No cross-fitting theorem is asserted here.
